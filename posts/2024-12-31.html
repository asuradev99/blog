Holidays are going well. I played many games with my family and we flew through a new TV series called Silo. I am also making a lot of progress in my thermodynamics textbook, and I have also had a bit of time to work on Edena a bit.

I also had a somewhat interesting debate with my father on the merit of the book The Mountain People by Colin Turnbull. The premise of the book is that there is this group of Ugandan tribespeople, known as the Ik, who, in Turnbull's eyes, are victims of a selfish, brutal, culture due to poor living conditions and famine. Turnbull documents these people with a mix of disdain but also an attempt at sympathy for their position, judging from snippets of the book I have read and reviews I have found online.

Neither me or my father have read this book in any meaningful capacity and thus the summary which I just gave is likely limited or misleading. But, regardless, the reviews from other anthropologists I have seen online have convinced me to put down the book, which looked interesting at first. This is because some of Turnbull's claims have been challenged by researchers who studied the Ik after Turnbull's account as well as the Ik people themself. For instance:

  • https://aeon.co/essays/why-were-the-ik-people-vilified-as-selfish-and-nasty
  • https://www.humangenerosity.org/setting-the-record-straight-on-the-mountain-people-an-open-letter-to-simon-schuster/
  • https://thenextwavefutures.wordpress.com/2017/02/08/revisiting-the-ik/

Now, my father argues that bad reviews of The Mountain People are not sufficient to warrant putting down the book, and, in general, bad reviews of a book are not sufficient to warrant discarding what the book has to say. In many cases I would agree with him. However, in this case I would not. This is primarily because

a) There is no way for me, not even remotely close to knowledgeable in anthropology, to verify anything that Colin Turnbull says

  • People have a natural bias towards beautifully or emotionally written text. There is no way to "analyze" what Turnbull is saying in such a way that we can objectively pick out what is right and what is wrong. Of course, this is in general impossible for most other fields, but at least some other scientific fields have much more reliable sources of verification, such as mathematical derivation, confirmation with experiment, corroboration
  • Colin Turnbull's account is perhaps the most well known and one of only a few accounts of the Ik people. Potential for cross-evaluating Turnbull's claims with the work of other anthropologists is quite limited, and, indeed, many anthropologists disagree with what Turnbull has said.

b) The consequences of reading the book and believing it when Turnbull is largely wrong are much greater than the consequences of not reading the book and the reviews being wrong. In the latter case, I have gained no useful information, But in the former case, I have gained a false impression of the Ik people, and that is far more dangerous than gaining nothing at all.

  • In general, this reasoning is based on the idea that positive statements such as "the Ik people are such and such" are much stronger claims (and thus much more dangerous) than negative claims such as "The Ik people are not necessarily such and such"

Point a) demonstrates a larger problem that I have with ingesting information with no easy way to verify claims. We like to think that we have the tools at hand to discern truth and falsehood in every situation or in every piece of information we read. We do not. There is some information for which we must ingest it through an inherently biased perspective, where there is no way to separate our own bias from the information itself. So, as my father pointed out, why should we ingest any sort of uncertain or "squishy" knowledge? This reasoning might lead us into a nihilistic void where nothing is certain and thus we should not try to investigate anything at all.

In general, I do think that if people would simply admit when they don't know as well as be less eager to jump to conclusions about something they have limited information on, I think the world would be a lot better place, even if it means trimming down the majority of our human knowledge. However, that doesn't mean we can't try to use the tools we have on hand to investigate anything. In general, in the case where there is plausible evidence for both sides of an argument, there are usually two possibilities: either claim X is necessarily true or claim X is not necessarily true. If there is a "symmetry" in the potentially dangerous effects of believing X but X is not necessarily true vs not believing X but X is necessarily true, then X is worth investigating. However, if there is an "asymmetry" (eg. one scenario is much worse than the other) in believing vs not believing X, then you should assume the option that does the least amount of harm. In this particular case, assuming that Ik people are not necessarily in the way that Turnbull suggests does the least amount of harm given that there is plausible evidence for this.

Of course, this leaves the question as to what counts as "plausible evidence", as well as what counts as "harmful" consequences - these are both very subjective things. However, I think that in most cases it is easier to agree on what is harmful or not, than whether X is true or not necessarily so. (I will elaborate on this later)
