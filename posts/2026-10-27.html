Harsh truths

  1. Someone, or something, will always be better, by whatever definition of "better" you pick. That now includes AI, which will most likely be able to do the research I want to do better than I could. And I will always be placed on some scale, whether grades, papers, or citations, and compared directly to others, and where I land will affect my social standing. It's exceedingly likely I'll never be at the top of that scale, and there will be people I know personally who rank higher than me, no matter how hard I try. I'll most likely be average among physicists, because most people are average among their peers.
  2. Being better doesn't guarantee recognition or impact. Fame and popularity depend heavily on luck and timing, on what's hot at the moment, and don't reflect the actual nature of research. And there is no guarantee anything I do will have any impact on the world, social or practical. A big part of why I love physics is that it can tangibly improve people's lives, but especially in computational and theoretical physics, nothing guarantees my work will ever matter beyond itself.
  3. Achievement won't cure the anxiety.
    • The step-function fantasy isn't real: there's no time t where, once I've achieved enough, the anxiety suddenly switches off and I'm finally happy.
  4. I can't learn everything. There will always be more to learn than I have time for, and that's true for everyone.
  5. Time spent worrying is time not spent learning, and it never comes back. The anxiety itself costs me far more than the thing it's warning me about.
