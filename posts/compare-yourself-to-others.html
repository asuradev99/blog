1. The desire to be the best

I think it is a safe bet to say that at some point, we have all felt the desire to be the very best at something, better than others. I have felt it quite strongly several times throughout my life, and still feel it today.

I certainly can understand and empathize with where this desire comes from. It feels good to be not just good, but better than other people at something. I remember the surge of dopamine when I would answer a question the teacher posed faster than anyone else and derived great pleasure from this, even though I didn't want to admit it to anyone else. In high school, it certainly inflated my ego when other people asked me for help on a homework problem.

Conversely, it also feels bad to feel stupid -- when someone else corrects a mistake you made, makes you look dumb in front of other people, or bests you in an argument or ability or competition -- even unintentionally. I know of this feeling -- there is like a strong clenching in the gut, the flush of blood to the face, and the adrenaline glands are slightly activated, a slight blurring of the senses. Our inner monkey (or at least mine) certainly does not like to be offended.

But there is also a kind of sadness that lingers sometimes -- the feeling of being worse, of being unable to measure up -- in terms of raw talent, skill, or work ethic. In these moments, our past achievements and efforts are washed away behind a cloud of frustration, embarrassment, and feelings of inadequacy, like there is some huge, insurmountable wall that blocked us from being where we could have been.

2. Why do we compare ourselves to others?

Why do we compare ourselves to others? I wonder what the answer to this question is from an anthropological perspective. Maybe it was evolutionarily acceptable in some way, in our hunter-gatherer times.

  • Comparison is how we calibrate ourselves. Festinger's social comparison theory (1954): when there's no objective yardstick for an ability, we judge it against other people. There's no absolute scale for "good at physics," so comparison is part of how we know ourselves at all, not a defect.
  • Ego and self-worth: status is relative. In primate groups, rank tracks access to food, mates, and safety, so caring about rank pays off evolutionarily. Rank is positional, so "good" isn't enough. That's why the craving is specifically to be the best.
  • Dominance vs. prestige. Henrich and Gil-White distinguish status gained by coercion (dominance) from status granted to people others want to learn from (prestige). The homework-help ego boost was prestige: gentler, but still about rank.
  • The scale mismatch. These instincts were tuned in small groups, on the order of a hundred people, where being the best was achievable and meant something. Now the same instinct runs against everyone at MIT, everyone online, and every famous physicist. It works as designed, but it's fed a population it was never calibrated for.

3. The hard truth

So what's the solution? Is there any escape from these ego-laden feelings? Are these feelings reasonable?

  • The standard advice, "don't compare yourself to others," can't really be followed. You notice who finishes the pset first, who asks the sharper question, who gets it without trying. The noticing is involuntary.

First I want to get the hard truth out of the way: there will, most probably, always be people who are better than you: smarter, faster, more charismatic, stronger, etc. There might be people who, no matter how hard you work or try, are still better, without even trying to a fraction of the degree that you did. I regularly encounter and talk with such people on a daily basis. Talent or privilege absolutely exists, and can and often does absolutely eclipse hard work.

  • No escape hatches. The usual copes don't hold up.
    • "They're good at A, but I'm good at B." This is what the idea of multiple intelligences often gets used for. But scores across very different cognitive tests correlate positively, and a general factor explains much of that. Someone better in one domain is often better in similar ones too.
    • "They're smarter, but I have more experience." Sometimes true, sometimes not. They may have more talent and more time in, and then the gap is just a gap.
  • Assume the worst case. Both copes depend on finding an axis where you come out ahead. Assume instead that you are better at nothing that matters here. Everything after this has to hold in that world, or it doesn't hold at all.

4. Skill vectors and the boundary of the unknown

However, I will argue that there is a way out. To explain this way out, I first need to set up an analogy. People's skills are like vectors in a high-dimensional space. They have a magnitude, which for the sake of this analogy you can think of as the overall "magnitude" of skill, but they also have a direction. And there are a practically infinite number of directions one's skill vector could be pointing. However, vectors can end up pointing in very similar directions, for one reason or another.

The boundary of the unknown looks like a surface in this vast ocean of a high-dimensional space. It represents the boundary between the unknown and the known, the solved and the unsolved, the explored and the unexplored. We travel in the direction of our skill vectors, eventually pushing against this boundary and ever-so-slightly expanding it in the direction of our skill vector. Sometimes, someone with a larger magnitude of skill vector will push in a similar direction to us, and do so much quicker and better than we do. And this might make us feel sad.

There are limits to how much we can change the magnitude of our "skill" vectors. Time, privilege, talent, work ethic -- these are all things which influence this magnitude but to a limited degree.

But the analogy says a few more things:

  • Similar directions are rarer than they feel. In high dimensions, two random directions are almost always nearly perpendicular. For random unit vectors in d dimensions, the expected squared cosine of the angle between them is \[ \mathbb{E}\left[\cos^{2}\theta\right] = \frac{1}{d} \] which goes to zero as d grows. Even people working on "the same thing" are usually pushing on different patches of the boundary, shaped by their background, tools, and taste.
  • The boundary grows as we push it. As the known region expands, its surface gets bigger, not smaller. For a ball of radius r in d dimensions, the surface area scales as \[ A(r) \propto r^{\,d-1} \] so the boundary grows much faster than the radius. It's only an analogy, but the point holds: every solved problem opens new questions. Progress creates frontier rather than using it up.
  • Direction is more in our control than magnitude. Comparison is almost always a comparison of magnitudes, and it ignores direction entirely.
  • Vectors add. Working with someone stronger can reach points neither of you would reach alone, when that's available.

If there is one thing I have learned at MIT, it is that there are so many problems worth pursuing that the number of paths or avenues one could possibly take far outpaces the amount of talent available. Of course there are exceptions to this that I am aware of, like in very theoretical areas with a low amount of funding (string theory, particle physics, some areas of very pure math, etc.), where opportunities are more restricted. Well, actually, this may be more of an issue of funding -- perhaps if funding was more available, there would be more problems worth pursuing that could actually be pursued.

What makes me sad is not that there are too many people working on interesting problems already, but that there is SO MUCH interesting problems, particularly physics ones, out there that in my lifetime I will only ever get to really engage with a tiny sliver of the vast ocean that is the sum total of our human knowledge.

Even super geniuses like Terry Tao, the so-called "Mozart" of mathematics, cannot hope to address even a significant fraction of all problems in mathematics.

5. Stable matching: the best available person

Another example is the stable matching problem: you might not be the absolute best candidate to be working on a particular job or particular problem, but it might also be the case that the people who are better qualified (bigger skill vectors) are currently occupied with another problem better suited for them. So you, in the current moment, may still be the best person for a particular job.

  • What stability means. Gale and Shapley (1962): a matching of people to problems is stable when no person and problem would both rather be with each other than with their current match. A stable matching always exists.
  • What it doesn't require. You don't need to be the strongest candidate for your problem, only for every stronger candidate to be matched to something they prefer. Better people can only work on a few things, and they pick what suits them.
  • It survives the worst case. This is comparative advantage. In Ricardo's example, Portugal makes both wine and cloth more efficiently than England, yet both gain when each specializes in what it's relatively best at. Being worse at everything doesn't make you useless; it decides where you're most useful.
  • Rejection is part of the algorithm. Proposers work down their lists, and getting turned down a few times before matching is normal. A "no" is a step in the process, not a verdict.
  • The matching changes. People move, new problems appear, the boundary grows. The problem you're the best available person for today, and the people ranked above you, will be different in five years.

There are so many problems that are worth pursuing, that don't have people pursuing them (e.g. D-Lab).

6. So compare yourself to everyone

  • Compare yourself to anyone and everyone. You're going to anyway. But not because comparison is knowledge.
  • Sometimes comparisons teach you nothing. Most of the time there isn't much to learn from someone who is just smarter. They might study in an objectively worse way, study much less, be less efficient or diligent, and still do much better. That's life.
  • The real benefit is practice separating the truth from the noise. Each comparison hands you two claims:
    • The truth: someone may simply be better, and there's nothing you can do to change that.
    • The noise: so you don't belong, so your work doesn't count.
    • The gut wants to collapse them, either by denying the first ("they just got lucky," "I'm good at something else") or by inflating the second. Every comparison is a rep at refusing both, until it becomes a habit instead of an effort.
    • Someone being better can be true, and also not matter in the way you think.
  • Occasionally there is something to learn, usually not their habits but their taste in problems, how they frame a question, or an explanation that clicks. Take it when it's there, but it isn't the reason to compare.
  • Real competitions are rarely final. Jobs, scholarships, and lab spots exist, but specific doors closing doesn't close the path. One lab's rejection doesn't stop me from doing physics.
  • Weigh the evidence honestly. One rejection is noise. A long, consistent pattern is information, but even then the question is "what should I adjust?", not "should I quit?" The bar for "this area isn't for me" should be very high, like being rejected from the vast majority of opportunities every time.
  • Two signals that actually matter:
    • External: let the world tell me no, instead of my own internal anxiety saying it in advance.
    • Internal: whether the joy is still there. That doesn't need anyone else's verdict.
  • I will keep pursuing the things I am interested in, not because I will be the greatest at it, but because I derive the most joy from it.

7. More ocean than swimmers

  • This is easy to believe at 5 a.m. right after the idea. It'll be harder the next time someone clearly outshines me on something I care about, and it might sting anyway. That doesn't mean the reasoning failed, just that I care.
  • The pocket version:
    1. Someone may simply be better. That's true, and it doesn't mean what my gut says.
    2. Learn from them when there's something to learn, but don't count on it.
    3. Doors close; paths rarely do.
    4. A pattern means adjust, not quit.
    5. I don't need to be the best person, only the best available one.
    6. Do it for the joy, not to be the greatest.
  • I'll never be the best at all of it, and neither will anyone else, including the people I compare myself to. But there is far more ocean than there are swimmers.
