I wanted to write down some thoughts about AI. AI's alleged proof of the Riemann hypothesis, even if it turns out to be wrong, has solidified my conviction that the old problem-solving pen and paper way of doing math research is pretty much dead. That is fairly obvious; what is not obvious is what is going to come. If we extrapolate this trend of AI expansion, what will be left for humans?

I am not, at present, depressed with the scenario that AI will leave nothing for humans as I don't know how plausible this is.

One thing which has struck me is, most people are talking about AI's ability to solve problems. But what about AI's ability to come up with novel, interesting problems? The RH didn't just fall out of a hat; it came from somewhere. There is very little exposition on the process of how people come up with interesting questions, conjectures, etc. That is likely because it is a very subjective endeavour. But, ironically enough, subjective things are more immune to being overtaken by AI. So I think any discussion of AI absorbing mathematics must also account for how AI will completely replace our ability to ask interesting questions as well.

Let's suppose the worst-case hypothetical scenario to test my conviction. Suppose there is some closed-loop AI system that can not only come with a proof of nearly any theorem at speeds which render pure human efforts impractical (solely for the purpose of determining if true/false), but can also generate new ideas/conjectures/questions which the human mathematics community considers sufficiently "interesting" to pursue, for some definition of interesting. It seems almost by definition that what is interesting is, tautologically, a function of what is interesting to the set of human beings, always, and therefore can never be replaced by a closed AI system.
