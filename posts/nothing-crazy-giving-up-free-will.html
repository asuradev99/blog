Let's think for a moment about what the world would be like if we believed in determinism and didn't believe in both free will and moral responsibility. Instead of rigorously defining free will, I want to instead capture the essence of how we intuitively use concepts like free will and moral responsibility in the first place. So I will be deliberately wishy-washy on what is meant by "could do otherwise". I will only stipulate that free will is synonymous with this intuition about "could do otherwise".

Free will is used to make sense of the choices we make on a daily basis. For example, you might believe that you freely chose between whether to have A or B for breakfast, and that had you had A for breakfast, you could have had B for breakfast.

However, suppose for a moment that you instead believe that, given the exact state of the world and the laws of nature, you could not have done anything other than eat A for breakfast this morning. Is there anything seriously concerning about holding that belief? I do not think so. The fact that you could not have eaten B or anything else even though you considered those alternatives need not be relevant to what you will eat in the future, even if the world is deterministic. Unless you possess something like an infallible oracle telling you that you will eat A for every breakfast to come, you remain uncertain about your future actions. You therefore still have reason to deliberate, consider alternatives, and respond to new information. The representation of your situation as a choice between A and B can remain entirely useful without requiring that both outcomes be genuinely possible given the actual past and laws of nature. It can function as an epistemic model: a way of representing your uncertainty about what you will do. In this respect it resembles prediction in other deterministic systems, such as weather forecasting, where several outcomes may reasonably be considered even if only one will actually occur.

Of course, differently from weather prediction, deliberation is more than mere prediction. Your consideration of A and B, your preferences, and your reasons may themselves be among the causes that determine what you eventually eat. But this is entirely compatible with a deterministic world as well: our thoughts, regardless of how "freely willed" they are, have a causal impact on the future. This is no different from a very complex black box algorithm, initially identifying two possible solutions to a given problem, deciding between which of the two to select given more information. To believe otherwise, that the same future will occur regardless of what we decide, is to commit the fallacy of mistaking determinism for fatalism. So giving up the belief that you could have done otherwise need not require giving up deliberation, choice-talk, or practical agency.

It is important to emphasize that our everyday model of the choices we are "open" to make is entirely heuristic, and a function of what we know. It is in no way a universal guarantee of a particular set of actions, abilities, or outcomes. We may end up doing something else entirely than the set of options we devised. It is therefore not a good standard to assign moral responsibility.

Let's also take guilt and regret as an example. We often feel deep frustration, sadness, more specifically guilt/regret over things that we have done in the past. A large part of this, intuitively speaking, is that we think we could have done otherwise: at that particular time of decision, there was some other option available to us, that we very well could have done, but we didn't. We have an intuitive understanding of situations where we could not have done otherwise, eg. accidents or forced behavior, from ones where we have some form of "genuine agency".

But let's suppose we drop this distinction. Let's suppose that, no matter what we did, we couldn't have done otherwise and were not morally responsible for it. Let's start with a simple everyday example: suppose I wronged a friend. Let's now list some of the intuitions we have about what kind of feelings we expect people to have when they violate some social norm.

  • Guilt: a negative feeling that comes when there is acknowledgement that you have done something wrong.
  • Shame: feeling of embarrassment that you did something wrong.
  • Regret: wishing that, at the time of the action, you had actually done something else.
  • Additionally, a willingness to right the wrong somehow / prevent it from happening again in the future.

But it is entirely possible to have all of the feelings above, except regret, and still recover most of our intuitions about blame. Recognizing the significance of the harm one caused does not necessarily require wishing that, at that exact past moment, one had done something else. I can acknowledge that what I did was wrong, feel shame or guilt about having caused harm, care deeply about the person I harmed, and want to repair the damage or change my future behavior, while nevertheless believing that, given the exact circumstances at the time, I could not actually have acted otherwise.

Of course, I do think that not believing in free will requires that we change our conception of when to apply guilt and shame, which are nothing more than behavioral tools. But unlike regret, I still think there exist certain cases where these behavioral tools may be appropriate, whereas in no situation do I think regret is appropriate, because we are assuming it is impossible to have done otherwise.
